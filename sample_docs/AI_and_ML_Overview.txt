Introduction to Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning

Page 1 — What is Artificial Intelligence?

Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to the simulation of human intelligence processes by computer systems. These processes include learning (the acquisition of information and rules for using the information), reasoning (using rules to reach approximate or definite conclusions), and self-correction. AI systems are designed to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as visual perception, speech recognition, decision-making, and language translation.

The term "Artificial Intelligence" was coined by John McCarthy in 1956 during the Dartmouth Conference, which is widely considered the birthplace of AI as a scientific discipline. Since then, AI has evolved through several periods of optimism and setback — often called "AI winters" — to become the transformative technology it is today.

AI can be broadly categorized into:
- Narrow AI (Weak AI): Systems designed for a specific task (e.g., voice assistants, image classifiers).
- General AI (Strong AI): Hypothetical systems with human-like cognitive abilities across any domain.
- Super AI: Theoretical AI surpassing human intelligence in all domains.

Modern AI applications include recommendation systems (Netflix, Spotify), fraud detection in banking, medical diagnosis support, autonomous vehicles, and natural language processing.

Page 2 — Machine Learning Fundamentals

Machine Learning (ML) is a subset of AI that enables systems to learn and improve from experience without being explicitly programmed. The core idea is to give machines access to data and let them learn from it.

There are three primary types of machine learning:

1. Supervised Learning: The model is trained on labeled data. For every input, there is a corresponding output label. Common algorithms include linear regression, logistic regression, support vector machines (SVM), and neural networks. Applications: spam detection, image classification, sentiment analysis.

2. Unsupervised Learning: The model is trained on unlabeled data and must find patterns and relationships on its own. Common algorithms include k-means clustering, hierarchical clustering, and principal component analysis (PCA). Applications: customer segmentation, anomaly detection, topic modeling.

3. Reinforcement Learning: The model learns through trial and error, receiving rewards or penalties based on its actions. It interacts with an environment and seeks to maximize cumulative reward. Applications: game playing (AlphaGo, Chess engines), robotics, autonomous driving.

Key ML concepts include features (input variables), labels (output variables), training data, test data, overfitting (model too complex, poor generalization), and underfitting (model too simple).

Page 3 — Deep Learning and Neural Networks

Deep Learning is a subset of machine learning based on artificial neural networks with many layers (hence "deep"). These multi-layered networks, called deep neural networks (DNNs), can automatically learn representations of data with multiple levels of abstraction.

The basic unit of a neural network is the neuron (or node). Each neuron receives inputs, applies a weighted sum, passes it through an activation function (e.g., ReLU, Sigmoid, Tanh), and produces an output. Neurons are organized in layers:
- Input Layer: Receives raw data.
- Hidden Layers: Intermediate processing layers (can be dozens or hundreds deep).
- Output Layer: Produces the final prediction.

Popular deep learning architectures include:
- Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs): Specialized for image processing. Used in object detection, facial recognition.
- Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs): Designed for sequential data (text, speech). Variants include LSTM and GRU.
- Transformers: State-of-the-art architecture for NLP tasks, powering models like BERT and GPT.
- Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs): Used for generating synthetic data, images, and video.

Deep learning has achieved superhuman performance in image classification (ImageNet challenge), game playing (Atari games, Go), and protein structure prediction (AlphaFold).

Page 4 — Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Natural Language Processing is a branch of AI concerned with the interaction between computers and human language. It involves enabling machines to read, understand, and generate human language in a meaningful way.

Core NLP tasks include:
- Tokenization: Breaking text into words or subwords.
- Part-of-Speech Tagging: Identifying grammatical roles of words.
- Named Entity Recognition (NER): Identifying names, locations, organizations in text.
- Sentiment Analysis: Determining the emotional tone of text.
- Machine Translation: Translating text between languages.
- Question Answering: Extracting answers from documents.
- Text Summarization: Producing concise summaries of long documents.

Key NLP milestones:
- Word2Vec (2013): Word embeddings that capture semantic relationships.
- BERT (2018): Bidirectional transformer model by Google, achieved state-of-the-art on many benchmarks.
- GPT (2018–present): Autoregressive language models by OpenAI, scaling up to GPT-4.
- ChatGPT (2022): Conversational AI assistant with instruction fine-tuning.

Modern NLP relies heavily on the Transformer architecture, introduced in the paper "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017). Attention mechanisms allow models to weigh the importance of different words when producing outputs.

Page 5 — Ethics in AI

As AI systems become more powerful and pervasive, ethical considerations become critical. Key ethical concerns in AI include:

1. Bias and Fairness: AI models can inherit biases present in training data, leading to discriminatory outcomes. For example, facial recognition systems have shown higher error rates for darker-skinned individuals. Mitigation involves diverse datasets, fairness auditing, and bias correction techniques.

2. Privacy: AI systems often require large amounts of personal data. Concerns include data collection without consent, surveillance, and re-identification attacks on anonymized datasets. Regulations like GDPR (EU) and CCPA (California) attempt to address these concerns.

3. Transparency and Explainability: Many deep learning models act as "black boxes" — their decision-making process is opaque. Explainable AI (XAI) research aims to make model decisions interpretable by humans. Techniques include SHAP, LIME, and attention visualization.

4. Accountability: When an AI system causes harm (e.g., an autonomous vehicle accident), determining responsibility — between developers, manufacturers, and operators — is legally and ethically complex.

5. Job Displacement: Automation powered by AI threatens to displace workers in manufacturing, transportation, customer service, and other sectors. Economists debate whether new job categories will emerge to compensate.

6. Autonomous Weapons: AI in military applications raises concerns about lethal autonomous weapons systems (LAWS) that can select and engage targets without human oversight.

Major AI ethics frameworks include Google's AI Principles, the EU AI Act (risk-based regulatory approach), the OECD AI Principles, and IEEE's Ethically Aligned Design guidelines.
